Pulse wave and the Windkessel effect

Each beat the ventricle ejects about 70 mL in about 0.3 seconds. A bit over a third runs straight on to the tissues during systole; the rest (nearly two-thirds) is parked in the stretched aorta and large arteries, then pushed onward by elastic recoil during diastole. Shown in slow motion at 75 beats per minute.

  • Purple marker: the pulse wave, a pressure bulge handed from segment to segment. In reality it travels at 5–10 m/s, about 20–50 times faster than the blood itself. Both are slowed here so you can follow them, but the wave still clearly outruns the cells.
  • Red dots: blood cells. Right next to the valve they surge in systole and almost stop in diastole. Further out, elastic recoil keeps them moving between beats, so flow gets steadier the further you go.
  • Wall arrows: pointing out = walls being stretched (storing volume); pointing in = elastic recoil (releasing it). Recoil starts in late systole, as soon as ejection slows below the runoff.
  • Branches: the arterioles and capillaries are drawn as a few vessels standing in for millions. Each one is tiny, but together they have an enormous cross-sectional area (capillaries about 1000 times the aorta), so blood slows right down there, giving time for exchange. Real capillary flow (about 0.3 mm/s) is far slower than shown.
  • Bottom of the top panel: pulse size (purple) actually grows a little along the large arteries, as reflected waves add to it, then is damped across the arterioles, so capillary flow is steady. Average blood speed (green) falls as total cross-sectional area rises.
